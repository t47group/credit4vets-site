Credit card or debit card? What federal law protects when you shop

Credit4Vets· November 13, 2026 ·5 min read

At the register, a credit card and a debit card feel like the same thing. You tap or swipe, and you walk out with what you bought.

The difference shows up later — when a charge is wrong, an order never arrives, or someone steals your card number. Federal law gives each type of card different protections, with different deadlines. With the busiest shopping weeks of the year coming up, it is worth knowing which is which.

This is not an argument for using credit cards. If carrying a balance is a problem for you, debit or cash may be the better tool. It is about knowing what protection you have, whichever you use.

The basic difference

A credit card spends the bank's money. If something goes wrong, the disputed money was never yours to begin with, and you are arguing about whether you owe it.

A debit card spends your money, straight from your checking account. If a thief uses it, the money is gone from your account while the bank investigates — and that can mean bounced payments, overdraft fees, and a rent check that does not clear.

The law reflects that difference.

If your card or card number is stolen

According to the Federal Trade Commission:

Credit cards

  • If you report a lost or stolen card before it is used, you are not responsible for unauthorized charges.
  • If it is used before you report it, your liability is at most $50.
  • If only your account number is stolen and you still have the card, you are not responsible for unauthorized charges.

ATM and debit cards — your maximum loss depends on how fast you report:

  • Before any unauthorized charges: $0
  • Within 2 business days of learning of the loss or theft: up to $50
  • More than 2 business days after learning of it, but within 60 calendar days after your statement is sent: up to $500
  • More than 60 calendar days after your statement is sent: you could lose all the money taken from your account, and possibly more from linked accounts

The FTC notes that the 60-day window also applies when only your debit card number is used and you still have the card. That is why checking your account regularly matters so much more with debit.

If a charge is wrong or an order never shows up

The Fair Credit Billing Act covers credit cards and other revolving credit. According to the FTC, it applies to billing errors including:

  • Charges you did not authorize
  • Charges with the wrong date or amount
  • Goods or services you did not accept or that were not delivered as agreed
  • Math errors

To use it:

  1. Write to the card issuer at the address for billing inquiries — not the payment address.
  2. Make sure the letter arrives within 60 days after the first statement with the error was sent to you.
  3. Keep copies of your letter and documents. Certified mail with a return receipt gives you proof.

The FTC says the issuer must acknowledge your complaint in writing within 30 days and resolve it within 90 days. While the dispute is open, you can withhold payment on the disputed amount and related charges, but you need to pay the rest of your bill.

You may also be able to dispute the quality of something you bought with a credit card, but conditions apply — including trying to resolve it with the seller first. The FTC page explains the details.

With a debit card, contact your bank or credit union right away about unauthorized charges and errors. The FTC also recommends contacting your bank if you paid by debit and an online order never arrived, and it offers a sample dispute letter. Protections for a purchase dispute with a debit card may depend on your bank's policies, so ask.

Store rules: shipping and refunds

Separate from your card, the FTC says online sellers have to ship when they say they will. If they do not give a date, they have 30 days. If they cannot ship on time, they have to tell you and give you the option to cancel for a full refund.

If an order does not arrive, start with the seller. If that does not work, dispute the charge with your card issuer.

Watch out for fake "card protection"

The FTC warns about callers offering "credit card loss protection" or claiming to be from your card company's security department and asking for your account number. That is a scam. Federal law already limits your liability for unauthorized credit card use, and you do not need to buy protection.

If a call about your card worries you, hang up and call the number on the back of your card.

Habits that protect you either way

  • Check your accounts often — weekly during the holidays, and right away if anything looks off.
  • Turn on transaction alerts in your bank's app, so you see charges as they happen.
  • Save your bank's customer service number in your phone, so you can report a lost card quickly.
  • Follow up in writing after any phone report, and keep notes of who you spoke to and when.
  • Never share your PIN, and keep it off the card and out of your wallet.
  • Avoid using a debit card on websites you have never heard of. If something goes wrong there, it is your checking account on the line.

If you use credit, pay it in full

The protections above are real, but they do not cover interest. A credit card charge you cannot pay off in full when the statement arrives can end up costing much more than the item did. If you are not sure you can pay the balance, that is a sign to use debit or cash for that purchase — and to shop with extra care.

A note on this guide

This is general information about federal law, not legal advice. Your card agreement and your bank's policies may give you more protection, and some details depend on your situation. Confirm with your card issuer or bank, and see the FTC's pages for the full rules.
