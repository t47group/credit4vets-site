Scams that specifically target veterans

Credit4Vets· October 5, 2026 ·3 min read

Veterans are targeted deliberately. The reasons are practical from a fraudster's perspective: benefit payments are steady and predictable, service records are often findable, and shared military language builds trust quickly.

None of this reflects on anyone who has been caught by one. These operations are professional. Here are the patterns.

Benefits-related scams

Charging to file a VA claim. Accredited representatives — Veterans Service Organizations, accredited agents, and attorneys — assist with claims, and VSO help is typically free. Be extremely cautious about anyone charging large upfront fees or taking a percentage of retroactive benefits. The VA maintains a searchable list of accredited representatives; check it before paying anyone.

"Update your direct deposit." A call or email claiming your benefits require updated banking information. The VA does not request banking details this way. Initiate contact yourself, through official channels.

Pension poaching. Advice to move assets in order to qualify for a VA pension, usually paired with an annuity or trust the advisor sells. It frequently makes the veteran worse off, can trigger penalty periods, and the fee goes to the person giving the advice.

Credit and lending scams

Guaranteed credit repair. Nobody can remove accurate negative information ahead of schedule. Advance fees are prohibited for credit repair organizations. A guarantee of removal is a guarantee of nothing.

Advance-fee loans. A loan approval that requires payment first — for "insurance," "processing," or "the first payment." Legitimate lenders deduct fees from proceeds; they do not ask you to send money to receive money.

"Use an EIN instead of your SSN." Marketed as a way to start fresh. It is fraud, and you are the one signing the applications.

Military-branded high-cost lenders. A lender using service imagery is not therefore a good deal. The Military Lending Act's 36% cap covers active duty, not veterans — so after separation, the rate is whatever the lender and your state allow.

Employment and education scams

Job offers requiring payment. For training, equipment, or a background check. Legitimate employers do not charge you to be hired.

Fake check overpayment. You are sent a check, asked to deposit it and forward part of it. The check bounces after you have sent real money, and the bank holds you responsible.

Aggressive school recruiting. Some programs target veterans specifically for GI Bill benefits with pressure tactics and misleading placement claims. Check accreditation and whether credits transfer before enrolling.

Charity and romance

Fake veteran charities. Names closely resembling legitimate organizations, especially around holidays. Look the organization up before giving, and be wary of pressure to give immediately.

Romance scams. A long relationship, entirely online, that eventually involves a financial emergency. These take months to build and target isolation rather than gullibility. A person who will not video call and needs money is following a script.

The patterns underneath

Almost every scam has at least two of these:

  • Urgency. Decide now, or lose the opportunity.
  • Unusual payment. Gift cards, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, payment apps. All are hard to reverse, which is the point.
  • Secrecy. Do not tell family, do not tell your bank.
  • Contact you did not initiate. They called you.
  • Payment before benefit. Money out before anything comes in.
  • Service used as a credential. "I'm a veteran too."

What to do

Slow down. Urgency is the tool. Nothing legitimate collapses because you took a day.

Verify independently. Hang up and call the official number you looked up yourself — never the one you were given.

Tell someone. Secrecy is a requirement of the scam, which is why breaking it works so well.

Report it. The FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov, the VA for benefits-related fraud, and your state attorney general. Reporting helps others even when recovery is not possible.

If it already happened

Contact your bank immediately — speed matters for reversibility. Place a fraud alert or freeze with the credit bureaus. Report it. And if identity information was exposed, identitytheft.gov provides a step-by-step recovery plan.

Then tell someone you trust. Shame is what keeps these losses hidden, and hidden losses tend to get larger.
