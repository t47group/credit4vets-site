Got a VA debt letter? What an overpayment means and your options

Credit4Vets· November 9, 2026 ·5 min read

Few pieces of mail cause as much stress as a letter from VA saying you owe money back. Sometimes the amount is small. Sometimes it is thousands of dollars, for a reason you do not understand.

The most important thing to know is that the letter starts a clock. What you do in the first few weeks can decide whether your monthly benefits are reduced while you sort it out, and whether forgiveness is even possible later.

Here is how VA overpayments work and what your options are.

What a VA overpayment is

VA describes a benefit overpayment as a situation where it paid you, or your school, more than you were eligible to receive. It applies to disability compensation, non-service-connected pension, and education benefits.

Common reasons VA lists include:

  • A life change that was not reported, such as a change in dependents or, for pension, a change in income
  • A change that was reported but not applied before the next payment went out
  • An error, such as a double payment

VA copay bills for health care are a separate kind of debt, with a separate process. This guide focuses on benefit overpayments.

First, check that the debt is real

Before you do anything else, confirm the letter is actually from VA. Scammers send fake "debt" notices too.

Do not call a number printed in a message you are unsure about. Instead:

  • Sign in to VA.gov and use the debt management tool to see the amount and status of any overpayment, or
  • Call the Debt Management Center at 800-827-0648, a number VA lists on its own site, Monday through Friday, 7:30 a.m. to 7:00 p.m. Eastern.

And as the FTC reminds people, no real government agency will tell you to pay with a gift card.

The deadlines that matter

VA counts these from the date of your first debt letter. Keep that letter.

  • 30 days — to dispute. If you believe the debt is wrong, VA says that submitting a dispute within 30 days of your first letter stops collection actions until a decision is made.
  • Waiver requests and collection. According to VA's waiver page, requesting a waiver within 30 days for education debts, or within 90 days for compensation or pension debts, stops collection — including late fees, interest, and other actions — while VA reviews your request.
  • 1 year — the waiver limit. VA says it can only consider a waiver requested within one year of your first debt letter. By law, requests after that must be denied.

If you miss the early windows, you may still be able to request a waiver, but VA may keep part or all of your monthly payment while it decides.

Your options

Dispute the debt

If you think VA got it wrong — the amount, the dates, or whether you owe anything at all — you can submit a written statement explaining why, with any supporting documents. VA accepts disputes online, through Ask VA, or by mail to the Debt Management Center.

Disputing the debt is different from appealing the underlying decision. If you disagree with the benefits decision that created the debt, VA's appeals options (Supplemental Claim, Higher-Level Review, or Board Appeal) may also apply.

Request a waiver

A waiver asks VA to forgive all or part of the debt. VA says it considers whether you can afford to repay, even with smaller payments over time, and your personal statement explaining why you should not have to repay.

You will need:

  • VA Form 5655, Financial Status Report, which lays out your income, expenses, and assets
  • A personal statement
  • An optional request for an oral hearing

The outcome can be a full waiver, a partial waiver, or a denial. If any balance remains, you can set up a payment plan.

Make a compromise offer

A compromise asks VA to accept a smaller amount as payment in full. It also requires VA Form 5655.

Set up a payment plan

If you owe the money and cannot pay it all at once, VA offers monthly repayment plans. Plans under five years can be requested online, by phone, or by mail. Longer plans require the Financial Status Report.

Ask about a hardship suspension

VA also lists a temporary pause on repayments for veterans facing financial hardship.

What happens if you do nothing

This is where VA debt can get much harder. According to VA:

  • VA may withhold all or part of your monthly benefit to pay down the debt.
  • Late fees and interest may be added to certain debts.
  • After 120 days, VA refers delinquent debt to the U.S. Treasury, which can add its own fees, take money from tax refunds and certain other federal and state payments, or send the account to a private collection agency.
  • Unpaid or unresolved debts may be reported to credit agencies, which can lower your score and make future borrowing harder.

Nearly all of that can be avoided by responding within the time limits, even if the only thing you can do at first is ask for help.

Getting your Financial Status Report right

The Financial Status Report is where many waiver requests succeed or fail. A few tips:

  • Use real numbers. Pull a few months of bank statements and list what you actually spend.
  • Include everything — rent or mortgage, utilities, food, transportation, insurance, child care, medical costs, and minimum debt payments.
  • Explain anything unusual in your personal statement: a job loss, a medical event, a family change.
  • Keep copies of everything you send, and write down the date you sent it.

A family member, a VA fiduciary, or an accredited VSO representative can also request help on a veteran's behalf. VSO help is free.

Not every debt is VA debt

VA notes that federal student loans are not VA debt, even if the loan was used alongside VA education benefits. Those questions go to the Department of Education or your loan servicer.

A note on this guide

VA's rules and time limits are set out in your letter and on VA.gov, and they depend on the type of debt. This is general information, not legal advice. Confirm your deadlines and options with the Debt Management Center or an accredited VSO representative.
