Holiday spending without the January debt

Credit4Vets· October 26, 2026 ·4 min read

The holiday season does not usually create debt in one big decision. It creates it in a hundred small ones — a gift here, a split payment there, travel, food, a card that was supposed to be paid off in December and was not.

By January, the bills arrive all at once. For households on a fixed income, that can mean months of catching up.

Late October is the right time to plan. Here is how.

Set one number before you shop

Decide on a total for the season — gifts, food, travel, decorations, everything — before any of it starts. Base it on what you have or can set aside between now and December, not on what you spent last year or what a card limit allows.

Then divide it:

  • Gifts, with a name and an amount for each person
  • Food and hosting
  • Travel
  • Giving and donations
  • A small buffer for what you forgot

Writing names down matters. Spending tends to grow when the list is in your head.

Pay attention to how you pay

Cash or debit keeps you inside the money you have. It is the simplest way to stay on plan.

Credit cards can be fine — and offer stronger fraud and dispute protections than debit — but only if you pay the full statement balance when it is due. If you carry a balance, holiday purchases start building interest, and the gift costs more every month it sits there.

Store cards with "no interest if paid in full" offers deserve extra caution. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that with deferred interest, if the balance is not paid off by the end of the promotional period, you can be charged all the interest that built up from the original purchase date.

Buy now, pay later

Buy now, pay later (BNPL) is everywhere at checkout during the holidays. The CFPB describes it as a type of installment loan that typically lets you buy something immediately with little or nothing down and pay off the balance over four or fewer payments.

It feels small because each payment is small. The risks to watch:

  • Late fees. According to the CFPB, many BNPL loans charge no interest, but most charge late fees for missed payments.
  • Affordability. It is easy to have several plans running at once from different stores, each pulling from your account on its own schedule.
  • Overdrafts. Automatic payments drawn from checking can trigger overdraft fees if the money is not there.
  • Credit effects. How a BNPL loan affects your credit depends on the lender. Check the loan documents.

If you use BNPL, keep a written list of every plan, its payment dates, and the amounts. If the list gets long, stop adding to it.

Lower-cost ways to keep traditions

Some of the best holiday memories cost very little:

  • A family gift exchange instead of gifts for everyone
  • Homemade food or baked goods
  • Experiences instead of things — a hike, a game night, a day trip
  • A spending limit everyone agrees to in advance

Most people are relieved when someone else suggests a limit. You may be doing your family a favor.

Watch for season-specific scams

Holiday shopping is high season for fraud. Be cautious of:

  • Deals on social media from sellers you have never heard of
  • Texts about "delivery problems" with a link to click
  • Requests to pay with gift cards, wire transfers, or payment apps
  • Fake charities using military and veteran language around the holidays and Veterans Day

The FTC accepts reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

If you already have holiday debt from last year

Start there before adding more. A plan to pay off last year's balance before the new one begins is worth more than any sale.

If you have several balances, pay the minimum on all of them and put extra money toward one at a time — either the highest interest rate first, which saves the most money, or the smallest balance first, which builds momentum. Either works if you stick with it.

Plan for January now

The holiday bills arrive in January, often right alongside annual expenses and the cold-weather utility bill. Put the expected total on your calendar now so it does not come as a surprise.

And if you can set aside even a small amount each month during the year, next year's holidays can be paid for before they arrive.
