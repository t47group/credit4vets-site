Gift cards are one of the most popular presents of the season. They are also one of the most popular ways scammers get paid.

The reason is simple. Once someone has the card number and PIN, the money can be spent in minutes, and it is very hard to get back. That makes gift cards close to cash, without any of cash's paper trail.

Here is how these scams work, how to buy cards safely this year, and what to do if it has already happened.

## The one rule that stops most of these scams

The Federal Trade Commission puts it plainly: no real business or government agency will ever tell you to buy a gift card to pay them.

That includes the VA, the IRS, Social Security, your utility company, your bank, and law enforcement. If anyone tells you to buy a gift card and read them the numbers on the back, or send a photo of it, you are talking to a scammer. Full stop.

## Who the scammers pretend to be

The FTC lists the stories that come up again and again:

- **A government agency**, like the IRS or Social Security, saying you owe taxes or a fine
- **Tech support** from a well-known company, saying your computer has a problem
- **A friend or family member in an emergency** — sometimes using a cloned voice that sounds just like them
- **A prize or sweepstakes**, where you have to pay a "fee" first to collect
- **A utility company** threatening to cut off your power or water today
- **A romantic interest** you met online who suddenly needs help
- **A buyer or employer** who "overpays" you with a check and asks you to send the difference back on gift cards

For veterans, add one more: someone claiming to be from the VA or a "benefits office," saying there is a problem with your payments that a gift card will fix. The VA does not work that way.

## The pressure signs

The FTC notes that gift card scammers usually:

- **Create urgency.** They want you acting before you have time to think or talk to someone.
- **Tell you exactly which card to buy and where**, sometimes sending you to several stores to spread out the purchases.
- **Ask for the card number and PIN**, or a picture of the back of the card.

If someone stays on the phone with you while you drive to the store, that is a red flag by itself. A real organization does not need to keep you on the line.

## Buying gift cards safely this season

Gift cards are still a fine present. A newer problem is card tampering: the FTC warns that scammers tamper with cards on store racks to capture the number and PIN, then drain the money once someone loads it. They do not even need to be holding the card.

Before you buy:

- **Buy from stores you know and trust.** The FTC advises against online auction sites, where cards may be fake or stolen.
- **Inspect the card.** Make sure the protective sticker is intact and the PIN on the back is not exposed or scratched.
- **If one card looks off, pick another** — and tell a cashier or manager. The FTC notes scammers may tamper with cards in batches, so you may want to try another store.
- **Keep the receipt and photograph the card.** You will need the card number and receipt if the card is lost, drained, or used in a scam.
- **Consider paying with a credit card.** The FTC points out that if the card turns out to be empty, a credit card dispute may help you recover the money.

## What federal law says about the cards themselves

Most store gift cards and bank gift cards have basic federal protections. According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau:

- A gift card generally **cannot expire in less than five years** from when the money was loaded.
- Inactivity or service fees **generally cannot be charged in the first 12 months**.

The terms printed on the card still matter. Some issuers will not replace a lost or stolen card, and reloadable prepaid cards that are not sold as gift cards do not get the same protections. Read the package before you buy, and give the receipt along with the gift.

## If you paid a scammer with a gift card

Act quickly, even if you are embarrassed. The FTC recommends:

1. **Contact the company that issued the card right away**, even if the scam happened a while ago. Tell them the card was used in a scam and ask for your money back. The FTC notes some companies may be able to return money, especially if it is still on the card.
2. **Keep the card and the store receipt.** Do not throw them away.
3. **Report it to the FTC** at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).

If you cannot find the issuer's contact information, it is usually on the back of the card or on the card company's website.

If the scammer also got personal information — your Social Security number, your VA login, or bank details — go to [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) for a recovery plan, and consider placing credit freezes with all three credit bureaus.

## Talk about it with the people you love

Gift card scams work best on people who are alone, rushed, and told not to tell anyone. A quick conversation with a parent, a spouse, or a fellow veteran can stop one before it starts:

- "If anyone ever asks you to pay with a gift card, call me first."
- "The VA, the IRS, and the bank will never ask for gift cards."
- "It's always OK to hang up."

None of this is about being gullible. These operations are professional, and they are built to rush good people into bad decisions.

## A note on this guide

This is general consumer information, not legal advice. Gift card terms vary by issuer, and some states add protections beyond federal law. If you have lost money, confirm your options with the card issuer and the FTC.
