Today is Black Friday, and Cyber Monday is three days away. For a lot of families, this is when the bulk of holiday shopping happens — and scammers plan for it as carefully as retailers do.

Two kinds of scams spike during these weeks: fake online stores, often found through social media ads, and fake delivery messages that show up while you are waiting on real packages. Both are designed to look exactly like the real thing.

Here is how they work and what to check before you click or pay.

## Fake stores and too-good deals

The Federal Trade Commission warns that scammers pose as real companies and run social media ads for brand-name products at unusually low prices. Clicking the ad can lead to a site that takes your money and then sends a counterfeit, something that does not match the picture, or nothing at all.

The FTC also points out that social media platforms do not always thoroughly vet the ads you see or the advertisers behind them. Anyone can pay to run an ad. According to the FTC, people reported more than $95 million in losses in 2025 to scams that started with a social media ad where they then ordered something — and since many scams go unreported, the real number is likely higher.

### Before you buy from a store you do not know

- **Search the seller's name and website address** with words like "review," "complaint," or "scam." The FTC recommends this as a first step.
- **Be skeptical of steep discounts on name brands.** A price far below what everyone else charges is often the hook.
- **Type the store's address yourself** instead of clicking an ad, especially for big retailers. Look-alike sites may use a slightly different spelling.
- **Look for real contact information**, such as a physical address and a working customer service number. A site that only offers a web form is harder to hold accountable.
- **Watch how they want to be paid.** The FTC says a seller who insists on a gift card, wire transfer, payment app, or cryptocurrency is likely running a scam.

## Fake delivery texts and emails

This time of year, many people are expecting packages, which is exactly what makes delivery scams work. The FTC describes messages that:

- Claim to be from USPS, FedEx, DHL, or another carrier
- Say a delivery was missed, postage is unpaid, or your address needs to be updated
- Warn that the package will be returned if you do not act quickly
- Ask you to click a link to fix the problem

The link leads to a look-alike site. According to the FTC, anything you enter there — a card number, your name, your address, login details — goes straight to the scammer. Some links also try to install malware or capture logins for banking, email, or social media.

### What to do with a delivery message

- **Do not click the link** in a message about a delivery you were not expecting.
- **Check your order status** on the website where you actually bought the item, or through your account there.
- **If you think it might be real**, contact the carrier using a phone number or website you know is legitimate — not the one in the message.
- **Report the text** using your phone's "report junk" option, or forward it to **7726** (SPAM). Then delete it.

## Pay in a way that protects you

The FTC recommends paying by credit card when you can. Credit card disputes can help with double charges, items that never arrive, and wrong or damaged goods. We covered the details, and how debit cards differ, in our guide to [credit and debit card protections](/blog/credit-card-vs-debit-card-protections/).

Whatever you use:

- **Save receipts and order confirmation numbers.** The FTC notes you will need them for any refund request.
- **Avoid using your debit card** on a site you have never shopped with before.
- **Never pay with a gift card** for an online purchase from a seller.

## If an order does not show up

The FTC says sellers must ship by the date they say, or the date in their ads. If no date is given, they have 30 days. If they cannot ship on time, they have to tell you and give you the option to cancel for a full refund.

If something you paid for does not arrive:

1. **Contact the seller** and ask for a refund.
2. **If that fails, dispute the charge** with your credit card issuer or, if you used debit, your bank.
3. **Report it** to the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).

## If you entered your information on a fake site

Move quickly:

- **Call your card issuer or bank** using the number on the back of your card, report the fraud, and ask for a new card.
- **Change your passwords**, starting with any account that used the same password, and turn on two-factor authentication.
- **Watch your accounts** closely for the next few weeks.
- **If you gave your Social Security number** or other identity information, visit [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) for a recovery plan, and consider credit freezes with all three bureaus.

## A shopping plan helps here, too

Scams work best on rushed shoppers chasing a deal. A list made in advance — what you are buying, for whom, and your spending limit — is one of the best fraud defenses there is. If an ad pulls you toward something that is not on the list, from a store you have never heard of, that is a good moment to close the tab.
