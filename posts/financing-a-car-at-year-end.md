The end of the year brings a lot of car advertising: holiday sales events, model-year closeouts, and offers built around a low monthly payment.

A good sticker price is only half the deal. For most buyers, the loan — the interest rate, the length, and everything rolled into it — decides what the car really costs. A great price with a bad loan is not a great deal.

Here is how to approach it, using guidance from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

## Start with your credit and your budget

Before you visit a lot, pull your free credit reports at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com) and fix anything that is wrong. An error that drags down your score can cost you a higher rate for years.

Then decide what you can actually afford — not just per month, but in total. Include:

- The loan payment
- Insurance, which can go up significantly with a newer or more expensive car
- Registration and fuel
- Maintenance and repairs

If the payment only works by stretching everything else, it is too much car right now.

## Get preapproved before you shop

The CFPB is clear that you do not have to get your loan through the dealer. Getting quotes from banks, credit unions, or other lenders before you shop may get you better rates and terms, and it gives you something to compare against.

Credit unions that serve the military and veteran community can be a good place to start, but compare more than one lender either way.

**Will shopping hurt your credit?** The CFPB says multiple inquiries for the same type of loan, like an auto loan, generally count as a single inquiry if they are made within 14 to 45 days of each other. Do your rate shopping in a short window.

## What you can negotiate

According to the CFPB, these are all open to negotiation:

- **The interest rate and APR.** Your preapproval gives you leverage. The CFPB notes the first quote may not be the lowest rate you qualify for, so ask for a better one.
- **The loan term.** A shorter term means less total interest. A longer term lowers the monthly payment but costs more overall and raises the risk of owing more than the car is worth.
- **Prepayment penalties.** Ask if there is one, and try to have it removed.
- **Optional add-ons** and their prices.
- **Your trade-in value.** Check estimates from independent sources, and compare the dealer's offer with what you could get in a private sale.
- **Some dealer fees**, such as preparation, documentation, or delivery fees, and any market adjustment above MSRP.

What you cannot negotiate: taxes, title, and registration fees set by state and local governments.

## Negotiate one piece at a time

The CFPB recommends negotiating the pieces separately. A dealer may give you a good trade-in offer and a bad interest rate, or the reverse. When everything is bundled into one monthly payment, it is hard to tell where the money is going.

Ask for the price of the car, the trade-in value, the interest rate, the loan term, and the estimated monthly payment — all in writing — early in the process. The CFPB offers a free auto loan worksheet to help you compare offers side by side.

## Focus on total cost, not the monthly payment

"What monthly payment are you looking for?" is one of the most common questions in a dealership, and one of the riskiest to answer first. Almost any car can be made to fit a monthly number by stretching the loan longer.

The CFPB's advice is to focus on the total cost of the loan. Ask: how much will I pay over the full life of this loan, including interest? Then compare that number across offers.

## Question every add-on

In the finance office, you may be offered extended warranties, GAP insurance, credit insurance, debt cancellation products, or extras like window tinting. The CFPB says these are **optional**, and their prices are negotiable.

Each one also increases the amount you borrow, which means you pay interest on it. Before saying yes to any add-on:

- **Ask what it costs on its own**, not just how much it adds to the monthly payment
- **Ask whether you can buy it later**, or from someone else
- **Check whether you already have similar coverage**, through your auto insurance or the manufacturer's warranty
- **Read the cancellation and refund terms**

If an add-on shows up on the paperwork that you did not agree to, do not sign until it is removed.

## A bigger down payment helps

The CFPB notes that a larger down payment reduces the amount you borrow and may lower your interest rate. It also lowers the chance that you will owe more than the car is worth if you need to sell it or it is totaled.

## Read before you sign

Before you sign anything, check that the final contract matches what you agreed to: the price, the APR, the term, the down payment, the trade-in value, and any add-ons. Do not sign a contract with blank spaces. Take your copy with you.

If you feel rushed, it is fine to leave. Year-end sales come back every year.

## If you are already in a car loan you cannot afford

Contact your lender before you miss a payment. Ask what options exist. And if you are thinking about trading in a car you still owe money on, find out exactly how much you owe — rolling negative equity into a new loan means paying for your old car and your new one at the same time.

## A note on this guide

Loan terms and dealer practices vary, and what makes sense depends on your situation. This is general information, not financial advice. Confirm terms with your lender and read your contract carefully.
