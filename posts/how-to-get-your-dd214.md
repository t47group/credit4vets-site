Today is Veterans Day. To everyone who served, and to the families who served alongside them: thank you.

It is also a good day to take care of one small piece of paperwork that sits underneath almost every benefit you have earned. Your DD-214 — the Certificate of Release or Discharge from Active Duty — is the document lenders, employers, VA, and state agencies use to confirm your service. When you need it, you usually need it quickly. A VA home loan, a state benefit, a burial benefit for a family member, a job with veterans' preference: all of them can stall without it.

If you do not know exactly where yours is, here is how to get a copy, and how to protect it once you do.

## Getting a copy is free

According to the National Archives, basic military service records, including the DD-214, are generally free for veterans and next of kin when the veteran left service less than 62 years ago.

The National Archives also warns that some companies charge for DD-214 research. You do not need to pay one. The government provides this service free.

## Where to request it

### If you are applying for VA benefits

You may not need to request it at all. VA says that when you apply for VA benefits, it will request your DD-214 for you.

### The National Archives (most veterans)

The National Personnel Records Center (NPRC) in St. Louis holds most veterans' records. You can request yours:

- **Online through eVetRecs.** You will sign in with an identity-verified ID.me account.
- **By mail or fax using Standard Form 180 (SF-180).** The form's instructions list where to send it based on your branch and dates of service. The NPRC's mailing address is 1 Archives Drive, St. Louis, MO 63138, and its fax number is 314-801-9195.

The National Archives notes that records requests cannot be made by email.

### milConnect (more recent separations)

VA says you can request your records through the Defense Department's milConnect site if you were discharged after these dates:

- **Air Force:** October 1, 2004
- **Army:** October 1, 2002
- **Marine Corps:** January 1, 1999
- **Navy:** January 1, 1995

You will need a milConnect account.

### Marine Corps and Coast Guard

VA notes that Marines who served from 1998 to the present and Coast Guard members who served from 2008 to the present can also contact their branch's personnel command. A request sent to the National Archives will be forwarded.

## What to have ready

The National Archives asks for:

- Your **complete name used during service**
- Your **service number and Social Security number**
- Your **branch and dates of service**
- Your **date and place of birth**, especially if you do not know your service number
- Your **signature and the date**

It also helps to say why you need the record and whether you want anything besides the DD-214.

If your records may have been affected by the 1973 fire at the NPRC, include your place of discharge, last unit, and place of entry if you know them.

## How long it takes

Processing times vary. The National Archives says the NPRC receives about 4,000 to 5,000 requests a day, and it asks people not to follow up before 90 days, since that can slow things down further.

That is the real reason to do this now. If you are planning to buy a home, apply for a benefit, or start a new job in the spring, a request made in November is in much better shape than one made the week you need it.

You can check the status of an existing request online or by calling the NPRC at 314-801-0800.

## Requesting a record for a family member

Next of kin of a deceased veteran can also request records. The National Archives defines next of kin as a surviving spouse who has not remarried, or a parent, child, or sibling. You will need proof of death, such as a death certificate, a letter from a funeral home, or an obituary.

## Keep it safe once you have it

Your DD-214 contains personal information, and in many cases your Social Security number. That makes it valuable to an identity thief.

- **Do not carry it in your wallet.** For everyday veteran discounts, a VA Veteran ID Card or the "VETERAN" designation on an Arizona driver's license or ID does the job without exposing your Social Security number.
- **Keep the original somewhere secure**, like a fireproof box or a safe deposit box.
- **Keep a digital copy** in a secure, password-protected place.
- **Tell someone you trust where it is.** A spouse or adult child may need it someday to apply for survivor or burial benefits.
- **Share copies only when there is a real need**, and ask how the copy will be stored.

## Beware of "records services" that want money

Around Veterans Day especially, you may see ads or get calls offering to "expedite" your DD-214 or "update your discharge records" for a fee. Before you pay anyone, remember that the National Archives provides the record for free, and that accredited VSO representatives help with VA claims at no cost.

If someone asks for your Social Security number or VA login to "look up" your records, hang up and go to the official sites yourself.

## One more task for today

While you are thinking about records, take ten minutes to gather the rest of the important papers in one place: VA award letters, insurance policies including VGLI, and a list of your accounts. If something happened to you tomorrow, would the people you love know where to find them?

## A note on this guide

Request procedures and dates can change. This is general information. Confirm current instructions with the National Archives, VA, or your branch's personnel command before you submit a request.
