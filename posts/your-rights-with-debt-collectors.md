A call from a debt collector can feel like an emergency. The caller may sound official, the amount may be unfamiliar, and you may be told you have to pay today.

You do not have to decide anything on that first call. Federal law gives you real rights when a debt collector contacts you, including the right to see the details in writing and to dispute a debt you do not believe you owe.

Here is how those rights work.

## Who these rules cover

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's debt collection rule mainly apply to **third-party debt collectors** — collection agencies, debt buyers, and law firms that collect debts for others. They generally do not apply in the same way to the original company you owed, like your card issuer or a hospital collecting its own bills.

## When and how they can contact you

According to the Federal Trade Commission, debt collectors generally:

- **Cannot contact you before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m.**, unless you agree to it
- **Cannot call more than seven times in seven days** about a particular debt, or within seven days after they have spoken with you by phone about it
- **Cannot contact you at work** if you tell them you are not allowed to get calls there
- **Must stop emailing, texting, or messaging you on social media** if you ask them to

The CFPB adds that if a collector contacts you on social media, the message has to be private — not visible to the public or your friends — and the collector has to identify itself if it sends a friend or contact request.

## The validation notice: get the details in writing

A debt collector generally has to give you a **validation notice** when it first contacts you, or shortly after. According to the CFPB, it includes:

- The collector's name and mailing address
- The name of the creditor you owe
- The account number, if there is one
- An itemization of the debt — interest, fees, payments, and credits since a specific date
- The current amount the collector says you owe
- Your rights, including how to dispute the debt, and a tear-off form you can use

Read it carefully. Is the debt yours? Is the amount right? Is it a debt you already paid, or one that insurance, TRICARE, or VA should have covered?

## How to dispute a debt

The FTC says to send a dispute letter **within 30 days** of receiving the validation information. Say that you do not owe some or all of the debt, and ask for verification.

Once the collector gets your dispute, it **must stop collecting** until it sends you written verification of the debt. If you do not dispute within 30 days, the FTC says the collector may assume the debt is valid — so do not let the window pass.

A few practical tips:

- **Put it in writing**, even if you also call.
- **Send it in a way you can track.** The FTC suggests certified mail with a return receipt.
- **Keep copies** of everything you send and receive.
- **Do not admit anything** or make a "good faith" payment on a debt you are disputing until you understand it.

## How to make them stop contacting you

You can send a written request telling a collector to stop contacting you. According to the FTC, after that the collector can contact you only to confirm it will stop or to tell you about a specific action, like a lawsuit.

Stopping contact does not erase the debt. If you owe it, the collector may still sue, and it may still appear on your credit report. But it can give you room to breathe and decide what to do.

## What collectors are not allowed to do

The FTC lists practices that are off limits, including:

- **Threats of violence**, obscene language, or repeated calls meant to harass you
- **Lying** about the amount you owe, or pretending to be an attorney or government official
- **Threatening arrest** or legal action that is not true or not intended
- **Adding interest or fees** that your contract or the law does not allow
- **Publicly revealing your debts**

A caller who threatens to have you arrested over an unpaid bill is a serious red flag — either a collector breaking the law or a scammer.

## Old debts and the statute of limitations

The FTC explains that collectors only have a limited time to sue over a debt, usually counted from your last missed payment. Once that time has passed, the debt is "time-barred," and it cannot be the basis of a lawsuit. If you are sued over one, tell the judge.

Be careful, though: the FTC notes that in some states, making a payment or acknowledging the debt in writing can restart the clock. Before you pay anything on a very old debt, get advice from a legal aid office or attorney.

## If you are sued

Do not ignore it. The FTC says to respond by the date on the court papers, either yourself or through a lawyer. A collector generally needs a court judgment before it can garnish your wages or take money from your bank account.

**About your VA and Social Security benefits:** According to the CFPB, a debt collector has to sue and win a judgment before it can try to take federal benefits like VA payments. Even then, when benefits are directly deposited, your bank must protect two months' worth of those benefits in your account. Having benefits paid by direct deposit is what triggers that automatic protection.

In Arizona, Proposition 209 also lowered the share of wages that can be garnished for most consumer debts and raised several property exemptions. The Arizona courts' self-service center explains the details.

## Scams that pretend to be collectors

Some "debt collectors" are not collectors at all. Be suspicious if a caller:

- Will not give you a company name, mailing address, or written notice
- Pressures you to pay right away by gift card, wire transfer, or payment app
- Asks for your bank login, Social Security number, or VA login
- Threatens arrest

Hang up, and contact the original creditor using a number you look up yourself.

## Where to report problems

You can report a debt collector to the **CFPB** at [consumerfinance.gov/complaint](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/), the **FTC** at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/), or the **Arizona Attorney General**. The FTC also notes you can sue a collector who violates the law within one year of the violation.

## A note on this guide

This is general information, not legal advice. Your rights can depend on the type of debt, who is collecting it, and state law. If you have been sued or you are unsure whether a debt is yours, contact a legal aid office or an attorney.
