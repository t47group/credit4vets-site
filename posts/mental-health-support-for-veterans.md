The transition from service to civilian life is genuinely difficult, and financial stress makes everything about it harder. Money problems and mental health are connected in both directions — stress makes decisions harder, and difficult decisions create more stress.

Support is available, much of it free, and some of it does not require VA health care enrollment.

## If you need help right now

**Veterans Crisis Line: dial 988, then press 1.**

You can also text **838255**, or chat online at [veteranscrisisline.net](https://www.veteranscrisisline.net/).

It is free, confidential, available 24 hours a day, and staffed by people trained to work with veterans — many of them veterans themselves. **You do not need to be enrolled in VA health care to use it.** Family members and friends concerned about a veteran can call as well.

If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

## Vet Centers

Vet Centers are community-based counseling centers, separate from VA medical facilities, and they are one of the most useful and least known resources available.

They provide individual and group counseling, family counseling, and bereavement support. Services are confidential and provided at no cost to eligible veterans and their families.

Eligibility for Vet Center readjustment counseling is broader than many veterans expect, and it does not require enrollment in VA health care. Combat veterans, those who experienced military sexual trauma, and several other categories qualify. If you are unsure, call and ask — that conversation costs nothing.

You can find your nearest location through [va.gov/find-locations](https://www.va.gov/find-locations/).

## VA mental health services

For veterans enrolled in VA health care, mental health services cover a wide range: individual and group therapy, medication, inpatient and outpatient programs, and specialized care for PTSD, substance use, and military sexual trauma.

If you are not enrolled, it is worth checking whether you qualify. Eligibility depends on service history, discharge status, and other factors, and many veterans who assume they do not qualify actually do. The VA's mental health page at [va.gov/health-care/health-needs-conditions/mental-health](https://www.va.gov/health-care/health-needs-conditions/mental-health/) is the place to start.

## Why we include this

Credit4Vets is a financial organization, and mental health support is one of our four service areas. That is not a stretch — it is a recognition of how these problems actually behave.

Financial stress does not stay in a financial box. It affects sleep, concentration, and relationships, and it makes the sustained attention that financial recovery requires harder to summon. Veterans regularly come to us for help with credit or housing, and what is actually blocking progress is something else.

Through partnerships with mental health professionals, we offer counseling, coping strategies, and a support network built around veterans' experiences. It sits alongside the business, housing, and credit work rather than separate from it.

## If you are supporting someone

You do not need permission to seek guidance about a veteran you are worried about. The Veterans Crisis Line takes calls from family and friends. Vet Centers offer family counseling. The VA's caregiver support programs exist for this.

Being the person who notices and says something is not intrusive. It is frequently the thing that matters most.

## A note on reaching out

Asking for help with money and asking for help with mental health both carry a stigma that hits veterans particularly hard, and for understandable reasons — self-reliance is trained into you, and it served you well.

It is still worth doing. Every resource on this page exists because these are common experiences, not unusual ones, and because they respond to support.
