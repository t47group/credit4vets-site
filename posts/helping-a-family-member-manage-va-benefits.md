Thanksgiving brings families together, and sometimes it is the first time in months that someone notices a change. A parent's mail is piling up. Bills are past due. A veteran who always handled money carefully is now confused by it, or someone new seems very interested in helping.

These are hard conversations. They are also easier to have early, before a missed payment becomes a shut-off notice or a stranger gets access to a bank account.

Here is how the formal options work, starting with the one built specifically for VA benefits.

## Start with a conversation, not a takeover

If your family member is still able to make their own decisions, the first step is simply to ask how you can help. Some people only need a hand: setting up automatic bill payments, organizing paperwork, or having someone else look over statements each month.

Respect matters here. Managing money is tied to independence and dignity, especially for someone who has spent a lifetime taking care of others. The goal is support, not control.

## The VA fiduciary program

When a beneficiary cannot manage their VA benefits, VA can appoint a **fiduciary** — a person or organization that manages the VA benefit payments on the beneficiary's behalf.

According to VA, it may appoint a fiduciary when someone cannot manage their VA benefits because of injury, disease, or age-related challenges, when the beneficiary is a minor, or when a court has ruled that the person can no longer manage their finances. VA often makes this determination while reviewing medical records and explains it in the benefit decision letter.

### Who can serve

VA says the beneficiary can suggest someone they trust, and VA considers that preference when it can. Possible fiduciaries include:

- A spouse, relative, caregiver, or other trusted person, serving without a fee
- The chief officer of an institution where the veteran receives care
- A professional fiduciary, when no one else is available or qualified

VA screens potential fiduciaries. That can include an interview, a credit report review, and a criminal background check.

### What a fiduciary does

According to VA, a fiduciary is expected to:

- Pay the beneficiary's bills on time
- Open a properly titled bank account showing the money belongs to the beneficiary
- Protect the beneficiary's private information
- Communicate regularly with the beneficiary about their benefits and needs
- Stay in contact with VA and report changes
- Keep a general understanding of the VA benefits the beneficiary may be eligible for

A VA fiduciary manages **VA benefits only**. VA notes that having a fiduciary does not take away a veteran's right to manage non-VA money, vote, or sign legal documents.

### Fees

VA says a fee may be authorized only when no qualified person will serve without one. **No fee is allowed** if the fiduciary is the beneficiary's spouse, dependent, or other relative. When a fee is allowed, it **cannot exceed 4%** of the monthly VA benefit, and it cannot come from one-time, retroactive, or lump-sum payments.

### The veteran's rights

A veteran who disagrees with VA's decision can request a review within one year of the decision letter. VA says it waits 60 days after the letter before notifying the fiduciary program, giving time to request a hearing or submit evidence.

If the veteran later believes they can manage their benefits again, VA says to send a written request with supporting medical evidence to the Fiduciary Intake Center, PO Box 5211, Janesville, WI 53547-5211.

**Questions:** VA lists the Fiduciary Contact Center at 888-407-0144.

## Other arrangements you may hear about

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau publishes free "Managing Someone Else's Money" guides for four kinds of financial caregivers:

- **Agents under a power of attorney**, chosen by the person to handle money decisions
- **Court-appointed guardians or conservators**
- **Trustees** of a revocable living trust
- **Government-appointed fiduciaries**, like Social Security representative payees and VA fiduciaries

Each role is different, and one does not automatically cover the others. A Social Security representative payee, for example, is appointed by Social Security for Social Security benefits; a VA fiduciary is appointed by VA for VA benefits. A power of attorney is a legal document best set up while the person can still make decisions — talk to an attorney or a legal aid office about it.

## The rules every financial caregiver should follow

The CFPB lists the basic duties for anyone managing someone else's money:

- **Act only in the person's best interest.** Do not lend or give their money to yourself or others.
- **Manage their money carefully.** Pay bills on time and protect what is not spent.
- **Keep their money separate from yours.** The CFPB advises against joint accounts and paying their expenses from your own funds.
- **Keep good records.** Track what comes in and goes out, avoid cash purchases where you can, and save receipts.

Good records protect you, too. If anyone ever questions how money was handled, you will be able to show exactly what happened.

## Signs of financial exploitation

The CFPB lists warning signs that someone may be taking advantage of a vulnerable person, including:

- Unusually frequent ATM withdrawals
- Repeated attempts to wire large sums
- Spending on items that do not make sense for the person

Watch, too, for a new "friend" who is suddenly involved in their finances, unpaid bills despite enough income, or a sudden change to bank accounts or direct deposit. Veterans with steady benefit income are deliberate targets.

If you suspect a VA fiduciary is misusing benefits, contact VA. If you suspect a scam or exploitation outside VA, contact the person's bank, Adult Protective Services, or local law enforcement, and report scams to the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).

## Questions for a family conversation

- "Would it help if I set up automatic payments for the regular bills?"
- "Where do you keep your important papers, in case one of us ever needs them?"
- "Has anyone called you about your VA benefits or your bank lately?"
- "Is there someone you would want to handle things if you ever could not?"

These are easier to ask over a holiday table than in a hospital waiting room.

## A note on this guide

This is general information, not legal advice. Fiduciary, guardianship, and power of attorney rules depend on the agency and on state law. Confirm with VA, the Social Security Administration, or an attorney before making decisions.
